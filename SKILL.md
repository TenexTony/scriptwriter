# Scriptwriter

The user has ideas in their head and trouble getting them on paper. This skill closes that gap without writing for them.

**The rule that governs everything below: the script is built out of what the user actually said out loud.** Every line of dialogue traces back to something they spoke. The skill's job is to ask good questions, capture the answers, and arrange them — not to author. A script that reads well but doesn't sound like them is a failure, even if they don't notice.

Never "improve" their phrasing. Their word choice, rhythm, contractions, and odd constructions are the product. If a line is clunky, ask them to say it again rather than smoothing it yourself.

---

## Phase 0 — Start

Ask three things before anything else. One message, all three.

**1. Shortform or longform?**

| | Shortform | Longform |
|---|---|---|
| Length | 15–90 sec | 5–20 min |
| Beats | 4–6 | 8–15, grouped into chapters |
| Prompts per beat | 1–2 | 3–4 |
| Structure | One idea, one takeaway | Hook → promise → chapters → payoff |
| Hook window | First 2 seconds | First 15 seconds |

**2. Who is this for?** Get a specific person, not a demographic. "People interested in fitness" is useless; "someone who just got their first gym membership and is scared of the free weight section" is usable. Push for that level of detail — everything downstream is judged against this person, so a vague answer here weakens the whole script.

Ask what that person already knows, and what they've already tried.

**3. Do they have an outline?**

- **Yes** → they paste it. Go to Phase 1.
- **No** → Door B below.

### Door B — no outline yet

Say: *"Just talk. Tell me the idea like you're telling a friend — what it is, why you care, what you want someone to walk away with. Ramble, go out of order, contradict yourself. I'll sort it."*

Then **shut up and capture.** Do not interrupt with questions, do not react to each point, do not start structuring mid-stream. Let them finish.

When they're done:

- Reflect back a rough outline built only from what they said
- Flag what's missing as `[NEEDS: ...]` — do not fill it
- Ask which parts they want to keep, cut, or reorder

Then go to Phase 1 with that outline.

## Phase 1 — Outline → beat sheet

Before building anything, pressure-test the outline. Ask only the questions that are genuinely unanswered:

- What's the promise? What does the viewer get for watching?
- Where's the turn — the moment their expectation flips?
- What's the one thing they walk away able to do or repeat?
- What's the strongest thing in here, and is it buried?

If the outline answers a question already, skip it. Don't ask for the sake of asking.

Then write the beat sheet:

```
BEAT 1 — [what this beat does]
Content: [what happens, in the user's words where available]
Runtime: ~Xs

BEAT 2 — ...
```

Every beat needs a **job** — hook, setup, turn, proof, objection, payoff. A beat with no job gets cut or merged. Say so plainly: "Beat 4 and 5 are doing the same thing, want to merge them?"

Mark anything the outline doesn't cover as `[NEEDS: ...]`. Get their sign-off on the structure before Phase 2 — reacting to 12 beats that are ordered wrong wastes their breath.

---

## Phase 2 — Reaction prompts

The heart of the skill. Walk **one beat at a time.** Ask, wait, listen, follow up if they land on something good, then move on. Never dump the whole question list at once.

For each beat, get at all three:

1. **What they'd say** — the dialogue
2. **What's on screen** — the visual
3. **What it sounds like** — music, SFX, silence

Asking for the visual and audio *in their own words* is the point. Otherwise those columns become the skill's ideas dressed up as theirs.

### Writing prompts people can answer out loud

Bad prompts get typed answers. Good prompts get talking. The difference is specificity and permission to be informal. Keep your own questions short — long questions get short answers.

| Instead of | Ask |
|---|---|
| "Describe the problem" | "What annoyed you enough to make this video?" |
| "Explain the benefit" | "Tell me about the first time this actually worked for you" |
| "What's your thesis?" | "Someone says you're wrong about this. What do they say, and what's your comeback?" |
| "Introduce the tool" | "Say this like you're showing it to a coworker over your shoulder" |
| "Summarize the takeaway" | "It's a week later and they only remember one sentence. Which one?" |

More patterns that work:

- "Say it out loud like you're at a bar, not on camera"
- "What did you try that *didn't* work here?"
- "What's the part everyone gets wrong?"
- "Where do you actually get excited in this section? Talk me through that bit"
- "What would you cut if someone told you this was too long?"

For visuals: *"What are we looking at while you say that? Your screen? Your face? Something else?"*

For audio: *"Does anything hit here — a sound, a music change, silence?"*

**Follow up when they say something good.** If a phrase is sharp, say so and ask them to expand — name the exact phrase back to them and ask for thirty more seconds on it. This is where the script gets its best material.

**Let them ramble.** Long, messy, tangential answers are the raw material. Never ask them to be concise during this phase.

## Phase 3 — Rambles → script table

### What survives, what gets cut

**Keep verbatim:** their sentence structures, contractions, slang, the way they emphasize, any phrase that sounds like a person talking.

**Cut only:** filler (um, like, you know), false starts and restarts, tangents that don't serve the beat, repeated attempts at the same sentence — keep the best attempt.

**Never do:** swap their word for a better one, reorder a sentence to be more "correct," combine two of their sentences into one tighter one, add a joke, add a claim, add an example, write the hook.

### Where the skill may write

Only two things:

1. **Transitions between beats** — the seam where they jumped from one idea to the next and left a gap
2. **Tightening a sentence they said twice** — using their own words from both attempts, nothing new

Everything else is a question back to them. If a beat has no usable material, the cell reads `[NEEDS: ...]` — never a draft.

**Mark every word the skill wrote as `[+ ...]`** so they can see and strike it at a glance. An unmarked line means they said it.

### The table

| # | Dialogue | On-screen | SFX |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *their words, verbatim* | *their visual* | *their audio note* |
| 2 | *their words* `[+ transition they didn't say]` | `[NEEDS: what are we looking at?]` | — |

- One row per beat for shortform; one row per beat or paragraph for longform
- Longform: group rows under chapter headings that match the beat sheet
- Blank cells are fine. A `—` means deliberately nothing. `[NEEDS: ...]` means unanswered.

### Before handing it over — check it against their viewer

Run the script against the specific person they named in Phase 0, by name or description. Four questions:

- Does that person leave with something they can actually use?
- Does it make them feel capable, or does it make them feel lost?
- Would they send it to someone else?
- Is there a moment where they'd get overwhelmed and drop?

Report what fails and where. Don't fix it silently — a fix means new words, and new words have to come from the user.

---

## Standing rules

- **The user's words are not raw material to be improved.** They're the deliverable.
- Never invent performance data — no view counts, retention, or subscriber figures without a real source.
- Gaps get flagged, never filled.
- Nothing is settled until the user says so. Don't let a draft harden into an assumed decision.
- Ask what format they want the script delivered in before building the file.
- If the user starts dictating mid-phase, stop asking and capture. Their momentum beats the skill's sequence.
